Fair-value model how the vig is stripped out to get a reference price

Every posted price contains the book's margin. To know whether a price is good you first have to remove that margin and recover what the book actually thinks the probability is. Different removal methods disagree slightly — most on longshots. Run more than one and treat a bet as real only when it survives all of them.

Arbitrage and middles taking both sides at different books for a locked result

An arbitrage exists when the best available price on every outcome, summed as implied probability, comes to less than 100%. The profit does not depend on who wins. These are rare, small, short-lived, and books limit accounts that hit them repeatedly.

Player props where soft lines actually live

Props are priced with less attention than game lines, so book-to-book disagreement is wider and real mispricings survive longer. They also cost more credits: one request per event, charged per market returned. Pick one game at a time.

Parlay lab what the ticket actually costs you

A parlay does not add risk, it multiplies margin. Every leg carries the book's hold, and the holds compound. This tool prices your ticket against what a zero-margin book would pay, and compares it to betting the same legs one at a time.

Closing line value the only feedback that works before results do

Whether a bet won tells you almost nothing — variance drowns it for hundreds of bets. Whether you got a better price than the closing line tells you the same thing far sooner, because the close is the sharpest number the market ever produces. Beat it consistently and profit follows; lose to it consistently and no winning streak will save you.

How the closing price is captured and why it is an estimate

The free tier cannot look up historical odds, and the provider drops a game from the feed once it kicks off. So every time you scan the board, this app re-reads the current price of every selection you have an open bet on and overwrites its stored closing estimate. The last price it sees before kickoff becomes the close.

That means the number is only as good as your last scan. Scan within an hour of kickoff and it is close to the true number. Bet on Tuesday and never scan again until Monday and the captured price will be stale and your CLV will read as better than it was. The app shows how long before kickoff each capture happened so you can judge it.

What this tool does and does not do

It does not predict games. There is no model here that forecasts outcomes, because nothing published reliably beats an NFL closing line. The Base rates tab shows why: since 1999, betting every favourite, every underdog, every over or every under all land between roughly 48.8% and 51.3% against the closing number, while a standard −110 price needs 52.38% just to break even.

It measures price disagreement. Books do not post identical numbers. When one book's devigged probability sits away from the consensus of the others, that gap is measurable and, unlike a prediction, it is verifiable at the moment you see it. That is the only edge this tool claims.

Where the measurable gaps are largest, in rough order: player props on secondary books, alternate lines, cross-market disagreement during news, and stale prices in the minutes after an injury report. Game-line moneylines at major books are the most efficient and least worth your time.

The honest caveats. Consensus is not truth — if every book is wrong together, the tool sees nothing. Devig methods disagree, most on longshots, which is why the worst-case option exists. Closing-line value is the only feedback loop worth tracking: log whether your price beat the closing number, because results take hundreds of bets to mean anything and CLV tells you sooner. Books restrict accounts that consistently take the best price. And every parlay in the Parlay lab will show a negative expected value unless a leg is genuinely mispriced, because that is what the arithmetic says.
